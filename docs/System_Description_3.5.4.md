## 3.5.4 The Intervention: Design and Implementation of the Researcher-Developed Software

The intervention artefact produced under Research Objective 2 is a bilingual (Sinhala and English), level-wise assistive mathematics platform developed for Grade 4 learners identified with dyscalculia. It was designed as the operational expression of the study's analytical framework, so that each of its principal features realises one of the four theory-derived independent variables set out in Section 1.9 (IV1 to IV4). Its content is aligned to the Sri Lankan Grade 4 national mathematics syllabus, and its structure follows the basic-to-advanced progression justified by the Defective Number Module hypothesis (Butterworth, 1999), which holds that remediation should rebuild numerical understanding from its foundations rather than assume it. The platform serves two roles in the study at once: it is the treatment delivered to the Digital Learning Group (DLG) during the implementation stage (Section 3.6), and its ordinary operation records the usage data required by the engagement measurement instrument (Section 3.5.3) without adding tasks that would burden the learner.

### System architecture and technology

The software was developed as a browser-based web application. The learner-facing client was built with React, while a server implemented in Node.js (Express) manages authentication, application logic, and data storage in a PostgreSQL database. This architecture was selected for reasons that bear directly on the conduct of the study rather than for engineering preference. Because the application runs in a standard web browser, it requires no installation and operates on the modest hardware and network conditions typical of Sri Lankan primary schools, which supports implementation within the ordinary classroom timeframe (Section 3.6, Stage 3). Because all interaction passes through a central server and database, every learning event is recorded automatically and consistently at the point of use, which is what makes the software-generated component of the engagement instrument feasible. Content is presented through picture-based illustrations and on-screen animations, and a reading-supportive typeface, generous spacing, and a low-glare colour scheme are applied throughout to reduce visual and reading barriers, since dyscalculia frequently co-occurs with reading difficulty.

### Learning content and level-wise structure

The curriculum content is organised into a sequence of modules that progress from foundational number sense to the standard Grade 4 domains (number and place value, the four operations, measurement, money, geometry, and data handling). A dedicated foundational module is placed first and is the recommended entry point for dyscalculic learners. It teaches the pre-arithmetic skills that these learners often lack, namely counting and quantity (number sense), recognition of numerals, the link between a numeral and its number word, comparison of quantities, number sequence, and the meaning of the mathematical signs. Each activity follows a consistent three-part cycle of teach, then guided attempt, then independent practice. The teaching phase is delivered as a short animated lesson that is narrated aloud and advances one idea at a time, followed by a supported attempt before the learner begins the scored practice. This structure implements the graduated, scaffolded support prescribed by the Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978) and keeps each task focused on a single concept.

### Operationalisation of the four independent variables

The design translates each independent variable into concrete, observable features of the platform.

Multi-sensory numerical representation (IV1) is realised by presenting every concept through three coordinated channels. Quantity is shown visually through animated representations such as sets of countable objects, ten-frames, number lines, and place-value displays; all instructions and item text are narrated aloud through the device's speech synthesis; and the learner responds by tapping or selecting on screen and receives an immediate reaction. These channels deliberately connect quantity, spoken number word, and written symbol, as specified by the Triple-Code Model (Dehaene, 1992).

Adaptive level-wise scaffolding (IV2) is realised through a graded structure in which each activity is offered at successive difficulty levels. Progression is governed by mastery: a threshold level of accuracy is required to unlock the next level, and the difficulty subsequently presented is adjusted in response to the learner's performance, so that each learner works just beyond their current independent ability. This operationalises Vygotsky's (1978) scaffolding principle together with the foundation-upward rebuilding implied by Butterworth's (1999) number-module account.

Bilingual delivery (IV3) is realised through a persistent language control that renders all instructions, narration, and item text in either Sinhala or English at the learner's selection. Because the verbal code in numerical processing is language dependent (Dehaene, 1992), delivery in the learner's stronger language is treated as an intervention feature in its own right rather than a matter of convenience, and the language chosen is recorded so that competency gains can later be compared across languages (Hypothesis 3).

Immediate feedback and reduced cognitive load (IV4) are realised by presenting a single concept at a time in short practice rounds, without time pressure, and by returning corrective feedback with brief spoken encouragement after every response. To provide on-demand support during self-study, the platform includes a voice-based study assistant. The learner can ask a question by speaking, where the browser supports speech recognition, or by tapping a suggested question, and the assistant replies in simple bilingual language and reads the answer aloud, explaining foundational concepts such as how to count, how to compare quantities, or what a mathematical sign means. The assistant functions as an always-available "more knowledgeable other" in the sense of Vygotsky (1978), offering the individual, on-demand guidance that fixed-pace traditional instruction cannot provide to every learner simultaneously, while the single-concept design and reduced extraneous load are consistent with Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988).

### Guardian progress tracking

To extend informed involvement beyond the classroom, the platform provides a read-only guardian progress view. Using the child's login code, a parent or guardian can view a summary of the child's learning, including the number of activities completed, levels mastered, average score, time spent learning, active days, badges earned, and a simple record of mood at the start and end of sessions. This feature supports the sustained, informed guardian involvement identified in the purpose of the study, and its code-based, read-only access is consistent with the anonymised, code-based login used for the children themselves.

### Data captured for the engagement measure

During use, the software records each learner's activity in the database and makes it available as a per-student comma-separated export for entry into the statistical software used in analysis (Section 3.8). The recorded fields correspond to the software-generated usage logs specified in the engagement instrument (Section 3.5.3): time-on-task, measured both as the duration of each session and as the active time accumulated within activities; the number of activities and levels completed; and the number of attempts and retries per task. In addition, the platform records the response time to each individual question, the learner's use of the study assistant, and a brief, age-appropriate self-report of mood at the start and end of a session that complements the motivation questionnaire. Together these fields provide behavioural evidence of on-task engagement and persistence for the DLG, which is compared with the equivalently observed engagement of the Traditional Learning Group in the test of Hypothesis 4. A teacher-facing dashboard summarises each learner's progress and engagement and generates the export, supporting both the monitoring of implementation and the visibility of progress.

Table 5 summarises how the platform's features map onto the four independent variables and the data captured for analysis.

**Table 5. Mapping of system features to the independent variables and the engagement data captured**

| IV | Design feature as implemented | Underpinning theory | Data automatically logged |
|----|-------------------------------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| IV1 | Coordinated visual, auditory, and interactive representation of quantity, word, and symbol | Triple-Code Model (Dehaene, 1992) | Interaction events; response accuracy per item |
| IV2 | Level-wise modules with a foundational number-sense stage; mastery-gated, performance-adjusted progression | ZPD (Vygotsky, 1978); Number Module (Butterworth, 1999) | Levels and activities completed; attempts and retries; scores per level |
| IV3 | Learner-selectable Sinhala or English for all narration and text | Verbal code, Triple-Code Model (Dehaene, 1992) | Language of delivery; per-item performance by language |
| IV4 | Single-concept tasks, no time pressure, immediate feedback, and a voice-based study assistant for on-demand support | Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988); ZPD (Vygotsky, 1978) | Time-on-task; per-question response time; study-assistant use |

In summary, the researcher-developed software operationalises the four theory-based design features as working components of a single bilingual, level-wise platform, delivers the Grade 4 curriculum content to the experimental group across the implementation stage, provides on-demand voice support and guardian progress visibility, and captures the behavioural engagement data that, alongside classroom observation and the motivation questionnaire, supports the comparative evaluation of effectiveness and engagement in Stage 4.
