## 3.5.4 The Intervention: Design and Implementation of the Researcher-Developed Software

The intervention artefact produced under Research Objective 2 is a bilingual (Sinhala and English), level-wise assistive mathematics platform developed for Grade 4 learners identified with dyscalculia. Rather than being a general mathematics application to which learning-difficulty accommodations were later added, the platform was designed from the outset around the specific ways in which dyscalculia disrupts learning, namely difficulty in recognising numerals, in understanding quantity, in connecting a numeral with its spoken word, in interpreting mathematical symbols, in recalling number facts, and in managing the working-memory load of multi-step tasks (Butterworth, 2010; Kucian et al., 2006). Each principal feature of the platform was designed to realise one of the four theory-derived independent variables set out in Section 1.9 (IV1 to IV4), so that the design is not merely functional but explanatory: it is intended to show why a multi-sensory, adaptive, bilingual, low-load tool should help these learners more than fixed-pace traditional instruction. Its content is aligned to the Sri Lankan Grade 4 national mathematics syllabus, and its structure follows the basic-to-advanced progression justified by the Defective Number Module hypothesis (Butterworth, 1999), which holds that remediation should rebuild numerical understanding from its foundations rather than assume it is already in place.

The platform serves two roles in the study at once. First, it is the treatment delivered to the Digital Learning Group (DLG) during the implementation stage (Section 3.6). Second, its ordinary operation records the usage data required by the engagement measurement instrument (Section 3.5.3), so that engagement evidence is captured continuously and unobtrusively while the child learns, without adding separate tasks that would themselves increase cognitive load.

### System architecture and technology

The software was developed as a browser-based web application. The learner-facing client was built with React, while a server implemented in Node.js (Express) manages authentication, application logic, and data storage in a PostgreSQL database. These choices were made for reasons that bear directly on the conduct of the study rather than for engineering preference. Because the application runs in an ordinary web browser, it requires no installation and operates on the modest hardware and network conditions typical of Sri Lankan government primary schools, which makes implementation within the normal classroom timetable practical and keeps the intervention affordable and replicable, in line with the study's aim of proposing a scalable local solution. Because every interaction passes through a central server and database, each learning event is recorded automatically and consistently at the point of use, which is what makes the software-generated component of the engagement instrument feasible. In effect, the same architecture that delivers the lesson also produces the objective behavioural record that the study analyses, reducing reliance on observer judgement alone and strengthening the consistency of the engagement data across participants.

### Learning content and the level-wise structure

The curriculum content is organised into a sequence of modules that progress from foundational number sense to the standard Grade 4 domains, namely number and place value, the four operations, measurement, money, geometry, and data handling. Because many dyscalculic learners reach Grade 4 without a secure grasp of the concepts on which arithmetic depends, a dedicated foundational module is placed first and is the recommended starting point. It teaches the pre-arithmetic skills that these learners most often lack, namely counting and the sense of quantity, recognition of numerals, the link between a numeral and its number word, comparison of quantities, the order of numbers, and the meaning of the mathematical signs. This ordering is a deliberate response to the nature of the disability: presenting multi-digit arithmetic to a child who cannot yet reliably attach the numeral "5" to a set of five objects would demand exactly the abstract manipulation that dyscalculia impairs, whereas rebuilding number sense first gives the later work something concrete to rest upon (Butterworth, 1999; Kucian et al., 2011).

Each activity follows a consistent cycle of teach, then guided attempt, then independent practice. The teaching phase is delivered as a short animated lesson that is narrated aloud and introduces one idea at a time, using everyday, picture-based examples such as counting fruit or comparing groups of objects. The child then completes a supported attempt, with feedback, before beginning the scored practice. This sequence matters for a dyscalculic learner because it removes the assumption that the child already understands the task, replacing it with a demonstration and a low-stakes rehearsal that build familiarity and confidence before performance is expected. It operationalises the graduated, scaffolded support described by the Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky, 1978) and keeps each task focused on a single concept so that limited working memory is not overwhelmed.

### How the four independent variables help the learner

The value of the platform for the study lies in the way each independent variable is translated into a concrete feature whose purpose can be traced back to a specific difficulty of dyscalculia.

**Multi-sensory numerical representation (IV1).** Every concept is presented through three coordinated channels at the same time. Quantity is shown visually through animated representations such as sets of countable objects, ten-frames, number lines, and place-value displays; all instructions and item text are narrated aloud through the device's speech synthesis; and the child responds by tapping or selecting on screen and receives an immediate reaction. The purpose of this coordination is to help the learner bypass the core numerical deficit by building, and repeatedly strengthening, the association between a quantity, its spoken number word, and its written symbol, which is precisely the mapping that dyscalculic children struggle to form unaided (Dehaene, 1992; Kucian et al., 2006). Presenting the three codes together, rather than expecting the child to translate between them mentally, reduces the demand on the very process that the disability weakens. The ten-frame is used deliberately because it lets a child recognise a quantity by its shape rather than by counting each time, supporting the growth of number sense.

**Adaptive level-wise scaffolding (IV2).** Each activity is offered at successive difficulty levels, and progression is governed by mastery: a threshold level of accuracy is required before the next level opens, and the difficulty subsequently presented is adjusted in response to the child's performance. This keeps the learner working just beyond their current independent ability rather than being confronted with tasks that are too hard, which is important because repeated failure is a principal source of the mathematics anxiety and disengagement that accompany dyscalculia. By ensuring that each child advances only when ready, the design allows a slow learner to consolidate without being left behind and a quicker learner to move on without waiting, providing the individualised pacing that a single teacher cannot offer to every child at once (Vygotsky, 1978; Butterworth, 1999). The gradual unlocking of levels also gives the child a visible sense of progress, which is intended to sustain motivation across the intervention period.

**Bilingual delivery (IV3).** A persistent language control renders all instructions, narration, and item text in either Sinhala or English at the learner's selection. This is treated as an intervention feature in its own right rather than a convenience, because the verbal code in numerical processing is language dependent: a child asked to work in a language they do not yet command must carry an additional translation burden before any mathematics can begin (Dehaene, 1992; Laabidi et al., 2014). Allowing the child to learn in their stronger language removes that barrier so that assessment reflects mathematical understanding rather than language proficiency, and the language chosen is recorded so that competency gains can later be compared across the two languages in the test of Hypothesis 3.

**Immediate feedback, reduced cognitive load, and on-demand voice support (IV4).** The platform presents a single concept at a time in short practice rounds, without time pressure, and returns corrective feedback with brief spoken encouragement after every response. These choices are intended to keep the demand on working memory within the child's capacity and to reduce the frustration that drives mathematics anxiety, so that effort is spent on understanding rather than on managing stress or holding several steps in mind at once (Sweller, 1988). To provide help at the moment it is needed, the platform includes a voice-based study assistant. The child can ask a question by speaking, where the browser supports speech recognition, or by tapping a suggested question, and the assistant replies in simple bilingual language and reads the answer aloud, explaining foundational ideas such as how to count, how to decide which group has more, or what a particular sign means. The assistant functions as an always-available "more knowledgeable other" in Vygotsky's (1978) sense, giving the individual, on-demand guidance that fixed-pace traditional instruction cannot provide to every learner simultaneously, and it does so without a child having to raise a hand or wait, which lowers the social cost of asking for help.

### Accessibility for co-occurring reading difficulty

Because dyscalculia frequently co-occurs with reading difficulty, and because a child cannot demonstrate mathematical understanding if the words themselves are a barrier, the interface applies a reading-supportive typeface, generous letter and line spacing, a calm and low-glare colour scheme, and picture-based answer options throughout. Narration of all on-screen text further ensures that a weak reader is not prevented from accessing a mathematical task. These measures are intended to ensure that the study measures numeracy rather than literacy, protecting the validity of the competency comparison.

### Guardian progress tracking

To extend informed involvement beyond the classroom, the platform provides a read-only guardian progress view. Using the child's login code, a parent or guardian can view a summary of the child's learning, including the number of activities completed, levels mastered, average score, time spent learning, active days, badges earned, and a simple record of mood at the start and end of sessions. This feature supports the sustained, informed involvement identified in the purpose of the study and is intended to help reframe a child's difficulty as a remediable learning condition rather than as laziness or low ability, since a guardian can see concrete evidence of effort and progress. Its access is read-only and based on the child's code, consistent with the anonymised, code-based login used for the children themselves.

### How the system captures the engagement data

During use, the software records each learner's activity in the database and makes it available as a per-student comma-separated export for entry into the statistical software used in analysis (Section 3.8). The recorded fields correspond directly to the software-generated usage logs specified in the engagement instrument (Section 3.5.3), namely time-on-task, measured both as the duration of each session and as the active time accumulated within activities; the number of activities and levels completed; and the number of attempts and retries per task. In addition, the platform records the response time to each individual question, which offers an indicator of processing fluency and its change over the intervention, the child's use of the study assistant, and a brief, age-appropriate self-report of mood at the start and end of each session that complements the motivation questionnaire. Read together, these signals describe not only how much a child did but how they engaged while doing it: sustained time-on-task and repeated attempts indicate persistence, faster responses over time suggest growing fluency, and the mood record gives a simple measure of affect. This behavioural evidence is gathered for the DLG and compared with the equivalently observed engagement of the Traditional Learning Group in the test of Hypothesis 4. A teacher-facing dashboard summarises each learner's progress and engagement and generates the export, supporting both the monitoring of implementation during the study and the visibility of progress for teachers and, through them, guardians.

|----|-------------------------------|-------------------------|---------------------|---------------------------|

In summary, the researcher-developed software operationalises the four theory-based design features as working components of a single bilingual, level-wise platform, and each component is directed at a specific difficulty that dyscalculia presents. It delivers the Grade 4 curriculum content to the experimental group across the implementation stage, provides on-demand voice support and guardian progress visibility, and captures the behavioural engagement data that, alongside classroom observation and the motivation questionnaire, supports the comparative evaluation of mathematical competency and engagement in Stage 4. In this way the system is not only the object that the study builds but the mechanism through which the study's central prediction, that a multi-sensory, adaptive, bilingual, low-load tool improves outcomes more than traditional teaching, is put to the test.
